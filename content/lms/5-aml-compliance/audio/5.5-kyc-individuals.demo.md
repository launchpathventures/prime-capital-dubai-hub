Let me show you two versions of conducting KYC with an individual client. Same client, same information needed — but very different outcomes.

The scenario: a high-net-worth British investor, Mr. James Harrington, is purchasing a AED 4.5 million apartment in Downtown Dubai. He is a non-resident, visiting Dubai for a week. This is your first meeting.

---

[DEMO: Weak]

The consultant says: "Alright Mr. Harrington, before we can proceed, I need you to fill out this form. It is our compliance paperwork. Can I get your passport, please?"

The client responds: "Sure, here you go."

The consultant scans the passport and starts filling in the form. "Date of birth... nationality... OK, and what is your occupation?"

The client says: "I run a few businesses. Import-export, mainly."

The consultant writes "self-employed" and moves on. "Right, and we need something called source of funds. Where is the money coming from for this purchase?"

The client pauses. "I mean... it is my money. From my businesses."

The consultant says: "OK, I will just put 'business income.' And there is another question here about source of wealth — I think that is basically the same thing. I will just copy what I wrote. Now, are you a PEP? That stands for Politically Exposed Person."

The client laughs. "No, definitely not."

The consultant says: "Great, I will tick 'No.' I think we are done with the boring stuff. Let me show you the apartment."

[COACH EXPLAINS]

Here is what went wrong:

First, the consultant treated KYC as a box-ticking exercise. "Fill out this form" and "boring stuff" signal to the client that compliance is a formality, not a professional standard. This undermines the entire process.

Second, he accepted vague answers. "I run a few businesses" and "it is my money" are not adequate responses for source of funds. For a non-resident purchasing a AED 4.5 million property, you need specific, documented answers — which bank account, what type of income, can they provide statements?

Third, he confused source of funds with source of wealth and copied the same answer. These are different questions. Source of funds is where the money for this specific transaction is coming from. Source of wealth is how Mr. Harrington built his overall wealth over his career.

Fourth, he accepted a self-declaration on PEP status without noting that this client will be screened through Cygnus Scan anyway. If screening returns a PEP result that contradicts the declaration, the consultant has no documented follow-up plan.

Fifth — and critically — the client is a non-resident who runs import-export businesses. Under Prime Capital's risk rating table, this client is automatically high-risk on two counts: non-resident status and self-employed with a high-risk business line (import/export is listed as high-risk). The consultant did not recognise either trigger. This client requires Enhanced Due Diligence, and the consultant applied barely standard CDD.

---

[DEMO: Strong]

The consultant says: "Mr. Harrington, welcome. Before we start looking at the property, I would like to walk you through our onboarding process. As a regulated firm, we are required by UAE federal law to collect certain information from all clients — this applies to every property transaction in Dubai, regardless of the amount. I want to be upfront that some of these questions may feel detailed, but they are standard procedure and everything is handled with strict confidentiality."

The client says: "No problem. What do you need?"

The consultant says: "Let us start with the basics. I have your passport here — British national, currently based in London. Can I confirm that you are not a UAE resident?"

The client says: "That is correct. I am based in London."

The consultant makes a mental note: non-resident — this is automatically an EDD case. He says: "Thank you. And your occupation — you mentioned you run businesses. Can you tell me a bit more about what your businesses do?"

The client says: "Yes, I have two companies. One is an import-export firm focused on industrial equipment — we supply to construction companies in the Gulf. The other is a consulting firm for infrastructure projects."

The consultant says: "Thank you. And both are UK-registered companies?"

The client says: "The import-export company is registered in the UK, yes. The consulting firm is a BVI entity."

The consultant notes: BVI entity, import-export, non-resident. All high-risk indicators. He says: "I appreciate you sharing that. Now I need to ask about the funds for this purchase specifically. Where will the AED 4.5 million be coming from?"

The client says: "It will come from my UK company account — the import-export firm."

The consultant says: "Thank you. So the funds will be transferred from the company account. We will need a bank statement showing the available balance and ideally a letter from the company confirming the source of those funds. I should also mention that because the purchase is through a company account, we will need to complete additional KYC for the company as well."

The client says: "That is fine. I can have my accountant send that over."

The consultant says: "Perfect. Now, separately from this transaction, I need to understand how you have built your wealth overall. This is what we call source of wealth — it helps us understand your financial profile. Could you give me a brief overview?"

The client says: "Sure. I have been in the import-export business for about 20 years. Started small, built the business up. I also have property investments in London — two rental properties — and some stock market investments."

The consultant says: "Twenty years building the business, London rental portfolio, and equity investments. Thank you. I also need to ask whether you or any close family member holds or has held a senior government or political position — this is what we call a PEP declaration."

The client says: "No, nothing like that."

The consultant says: "Thank you. I will note your declaration here. As standard procedure, all clients are also screened through our compliance system, which checks against international databases. If anything comes up, I will discuss it with you. One more question — will you be the sole beneficial owner of this property, or will any other parties have an interest?"

The client says: "Just me. Personal investment."

The consultant says: "Understood. So to summarise what I will need from you: your passport copy, which I have; a bank statement from the UK company showing available funds; a letter confirming the company as the source; and I will send you the KYC form to complete. Because the funds are coming from a corporate account, we will also need the company's trade registration and identification for any shareholders holding 25 percent or more. I will email you a checklist so nothing is missed."

[COACH EXPLAINS]

Here is what made this effective:

First, the consultant framed compliance professionally from the start. "Required by UAE federal law" and "applies to every transaction" normalise the process. The client never felt targeted or accused.

Second, he asked probing follow-up questions. "Can you tell me more about what your businesses do?" elicited critical information — import-export, a BVI entity — that would have been missed with a checkbox approach.

Third, he clearly separated source of funds from source of wealth. "Where will the AED 4.5 million be coming from?" versus "How have you built your wealth overall?" are distinct questions that got distinct, useful answers.

Fourth, he identified the high-risk indicators: non-resident, self-employed, import-export (listed as high-risk entity type in Prime Capital's table), BVI company, and corporate funds. He recognised this as an EDD case without needing to consult a manual.

Fifth, he proactively told the client about screening. "All clients are screened through our compliance system" sets expectations and avoids surprise if screening returns a result that requires follow-up.

Sixth, he documented the document requirements clearly and offered to send a checklist. This is professional, thorough, and ensures nothing is forgotten.

[KEY INSIGHT]

The difference between these two approaches is not just compliance quality — it is client perception. The weak approach made compliance feel like a nuisance. The strong approach made it feel like professional standard practice. High-net-worth clients actually respect thorough compliance — it signals that the firm is serious, regulated, and trustworthy. The consultant who says "let me skip the boring stuff" is the consultant that sophisticated investors do not trust.

[PRACTICE TIP]

Before your next client meeting, review the individual KYC form fields. Know them well enough that you can ask the questions conversationally, not by reading from a form. The best compliance conversations feel like professional dialogue, not interrogation.
