Let me show you two versions of handling a sanctions screening result. The scenario: during a routine KYC review for an existing client, Mr. Hassan Al-Mahdi, Cygnus Scan returns a partial name match against the UN Consolidated List. The matched entry is "Hassan Al-Mahdi" — same name, same nationality (Iraqi), but different date of birth. The client has been transacting with Prime Capital for 18 months with no prior issues.

---

[DEMO: Weak]

The consultant sees the screening alert and thinks: "This has to be a false positive. We have been working with Mr. Al-Mahdi for over a year. He is a legitimate businessman — owns a restaurant chain in Dubai. The date of birth does not even match."

The consultant clears the alert in the system and notes: "False positive — DOB mismatch. Client known to firm for 18 months."

He does not inform the Compliance Officer. He does not suspend any transactions. He continues normal business with the client.

Two months later, a regulatory audit reviews the firm's screening records. The auditor identifies the cleared alert and asks: "Who authorised this false positive determination? Where is the analysis documentation? Why was no PNMR filed?"

The consultant cannot produce adequate documentation. The auditor notes a compliance failure.

[COACH EXPLAINS]

This is a serious compliance failure, despite the consultant's good intentions.

First, a partial name match — where the name and nationality match but the DOB differs — is NOT automatically a false positive. It is a partial match. Different dates of birth can result from data entry errors, transliteration issues, or even deliberate use of false identity documents. You cannot dismiss it on one data point.

Second, the consultant made the false positive determination unilaterally. At Prime Capital, only the Compliance Officer can make this determination after thorough analysis. The consultant's role is to escalate, not to adjudicate.

Third, no transactions were suspended. For a partial match, transactions must be suspended immediately pending resolution.

Fourth, no PNMR was filed. A partial match against the UN Consolidated List requires a PNMR via goAML within 5 business days.

Fifth, the documentation was inadequate. Even if this were a genuine false positive, the analysis, rationale, and determination must be documented thoroughly — not with a one-line note.

---

[DEMO: Strong]

The consultant sees the screening alert and reads the details carefully. Partial match: name "Hassan Al-Mahdi" and nationality "Iraqi" match a UN Consolidated List entry. Date of birth differs — the listed person's DOB is 1965, their client's DOB is 1978.

The consultant thinks: "This is a partial match, not a confirmed match and not an automatic false positive. I need to escalate this."

The consultant immediately takes three actions:

First, he suspends any pending transactions for Mr. Al-Mahdi. There is a property closing scheduled for next week — he contacts the relevant parties and delays without giving a specific reason.

Second, he compiles the screening alert details: the client's full name, date of birth, nationality, passport number, Emirates ID, last known address, and the corresponding details from the UN Consolidated List entry. He notes the matching identifiers (name, nationality) and the non-matching identifier (DOB).

Third, he contacts the Compliance Officer directly. "I have a partial name match from our periodic screening. The client is Hassan Al-Mahdi, Iraqi national, an existing client of 18 months. Name and nationality match a UN Consolidated List entry but the date of birth differs. I have suspended pending transactions and compiled the comparison. Can you review?"

The Compliance Officer reviews the case. She examines the full UN listing — which includes not just the name and DOB but also aliases, known addresses, and other identifying information. She cross-references the client's passport number and Emirates ID against the listed person's known identifiers.

The CO says: "The passport number does not match, and the listed person's known aliases do not include our client's known names. However, this is a partial match and we cannot dismiss it unilaterally. I am filing a PNMR via goAML within the 5-business-day window. Transactions remain suspended until EOCN responds."

The consultant says: "Understood. What do I tell the client about the delay?"

The CO says: "Tell him there is a routine compliance review that requires additional processing time. Do not mention the screening result, the UN list, or the PNMR. Standard processing delay language only."

The consultant contacts Mr. Al-Mahdi: "Good morning. I wanted to let you know that the closing scheduled for next week will need to be postponed slightly — we have a routine compliance review that requires a bit more time. I expect we will have an update within two weeks. I apologise for the inconvenience."

Ten days later, the EOCN responds to the PNMR: false positive confirmed. The listed person is a different individual with the same name.

The CO notifies the consultant: "False positive confirmed by EOCN. Lift the suspension and proceed with the closing."

The consultant contacts Mr. Al-Mahdi: "Good news — the compliance review is complete and we can proceed with the closing. Shall we reschedule for this week?"

[COACH EXPLAINS]

Here is what made this effective:

First, the consultant did not make the determination himself. He recognised the partial match, suspended transactions, and escalated to the Compliance Officer. The determination of false positive versus confirmed match is the CO's responsibility — and in this case, the EOCN's.

Second, transactions were suspended immediately. This is mandatory for partial matches. The 5-business-day PNMR deadline started running from the moment the partial match was identified.

Third, the consultant compiled all relevant comparison data before escalating. Name, DOB, nationality, passport number, Emirates ID — everything the CO needed to assess the match.

Fourth, when communicating with the client about the delay, the consultant used neutral language. "Routine compliance review" is factual and non-alarming. He did not mention screening, UN lists, sanctions, or any details that could constitute tipping off.

Fifth, the process followed the correct sequence: identify → suspend → escalate → PNMR → wait for EOCN → act on response. No shortcuts. No assumptions.

[KEY INSIGHT]

The temptation to dismiss partial matches as false positives is strong — especially when you know the client. But the screening process exists precisely because you cannot always tell who someone is from personal experience. The 18-month relationship and the restaurant business are irrelevant to the screening analysis. What matters is whether the identifiers match a designated person. Let the system work.

[PRACTICE TIP]

When a screening alert appears, before you do anything else, take a screenshot or print the alert details. Then immediately suspend any pending transactions. These two actions — preserve the evidence and stop the activity — give the Compliance Officer what they need to take the next step. Everything after that is the CO's domain.
